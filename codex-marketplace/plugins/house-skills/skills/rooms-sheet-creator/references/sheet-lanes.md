# Sheet lanes

Use the investigation packet as the source of truth for what can become participant-facing text.

## Prompt lane

A prompt sheet asks the respondent about their own participation, rooms, relationships, timeline/room split, memory,
materials, and boundaries.

Default structure:

1. Purpose and permission note.
2. Introduction for the respondent.
3. First contact and getting pulled in.
4. Rooms remembered.
5. How rooms worked.
6. Timeline versus rooms.
7. Creativity, jokes, drafting, stories, or exchange surfaces.
8. People remembered.
9. Bonds, friendship, support, and check-ins.
10. Drama, conflict, trust, and fallout.
11. Target-specific room, event, person, or exchange sections.
12. Movement, loss, disappearance, and migration.
13. Culture, ritual, and shared behaviour.
14. The feeling.
15. Memory fragments.
16. Optional materials.
17. Boundaries and use permissions.
18. The respondent's own role.

Customize by adding target-specific sections and memory anchors. Do not turn the sheet into a character brief. Convert
interpretive source material into open memory prompts.

Bad:
"You were the stable mirror to Cunty. Explain how you changed him."

Better:
"What do you remember about Cunty in rooms compared with on the timeline? Did he seem different privately? Did that
change over time?"

## Peek lane

A peek is a participant-facing character-read preview about the respondent. It is not a prompt sheet.

Canonical shape:

1. Short intro: rough current read, not definitive, invites correction.
2. Direct `[Name] is ...` statements.
3. Concise correction questions.

Use this pattern:

```text
[Name] sneak peek

A rough early read of [Name] as they currently appear in the project. This is not definitive. The useful question is:
what feels right, what feels off, what is too neat, and what is missing?

[Name] is ...

[Name] is ...

[Name] is ...

Correction questions:

What feels like you?
What feels off?
What is too neat?
What is missing?
What is true but phrased wrong?
What would someone misunderstand about you from this version?
```

Avoid writerly/process framing such as "as a writer" or "what I am trying to do." Do not ask full room, DM, conflict,
or exchange memory prompts in a peek.

For harm-bearing subjects, use a stricter correction axis if Harley approves showing a peek: accurate, overstated, too
flattering, too monstrous, too neat, missing context, and whether it excuses too much. Do not use a warm flattering
register when harmlessness, charm, or trust is part of the harm mechanism.

## Recall lane

A recall sheet asks a respondent what they remember about someone or something else. It may target a person, room,
event, drama, reputation pattern, reveal, fallout, or community impact.

Use recall when the target subject is not the respondent, or when the useful witness is the world around the subject.
Examples:

- "Tell me your version of the Evil Dave story."
- "What do you remember about Ku?"
- "What did people hear about Haiku Platypus at the time?"
- "What do you remember about Storytime as a room?"

Recall sheets must preserve basis boundaries. Include prompts that let the respondent distinguish:

- direct experience;
- heard at the time;
- rumour;
- later reconstruction;
- current interpretation;
- things they are unsure about;
- things they do not want attributed.

Default recall structure:

1. Purpose and permission note.
2. Subject anchor: who or what the sheet is about.
3. Direct memory.
4. Public/timeline impression.
5. Room/private impression, if relevant.
6. What they heard at the time.
7. What changed later or after a reveal/fallout.
8. Who was surprised, unsurprised, quiet, defensive, hurt, or recalibrating.
9. What the community got wrong.
10. What the subject seemed to need, want, protect, or get from the community.
11. Impact on rooms, relationships, trust, humour, safety, or memory.
12. Uncertainty, attribution, and boundaries.

## Harm-bearing recall variant

Use this for antagonistic or harm-bearing subjects. The lane is still recall, not prosecution.

Canonical principle:

Need is not excuse. Need explains function, not innocence.

When evidence strongly supports harmful or sketchy actions, do not ask the respondent to relitigate whether the action
happened. Ask what the behaviour did for the subject, what need it fulfilled, what room/social mechanism made it
possible, who noticed earlier, what was minimised, and what changed after fallout.

Useful need prompts:

- What did this person seem to get from being trusted, liked, feared, desired, central, harmless, or forgiven?
- What did access to rooms, DMs, women, status, attention, or ambiguity give them?
- What did people notice before the wider community did?
- What did people joke about, minimise, ignore, defend, or quietly warn each other about?
- What changed when the pattern became visible?
- What did the fallout take from them?

Do not make recall a gossip net. Do not ask for unbounded naming. Keep direct experience, second-hand memory, rumour,
and later interpretation separate.
