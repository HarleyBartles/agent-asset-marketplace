# Internal frames, occlusion, and off-frame space

An internal frame is a boundary inside the outer frame: doorway, window, arch,
mirror, reflection, screen, foliage, shadow, body, light pool, panel, or aperture.
Use it when the boundary performs a story job:

- isolate a subject or detail from competing information;
- establish an observer, witness, or surveillance position;
- confine a body or make a threshold legible;
- divide knowledge between observer and observed;
- connect near and far spaces through an opening;
- authenticate a viewpoint by showing the place from which it is seen;
- create an inset or nested voice without losing the containing field.

Repoussoir—the partial foreground shape that frames the scene—can provide entry,
scale, obstruction, intimacy, or a sense of looking past something. Occlusion can
also make a reveal, but preserve a clue, edge, gaze, shadow, or sound analogue that
lets the audience form the intended hypothesis.

## The outer boundary stays active

Off-frame space is not missing content. Gaze, gesture, motion, shadow, truncated
objects, empty direction, reflections, and interrupted lines can make the audience
infer an unseen person, cause, destination, or threat. State whether the off-frame
space should feel open, occupied, approaching, absent, or unknowable.

Use edge pressure deliberately:

- leave a subject room to look or move toward when continuation matters;
- crop into the edge when pressure, interruption, or bodily proximity matters;
- let a vector exit when anticipation is useful;
- let a vector return when the audience needs closure or orientation.

## Internal frame brief

Record the outer frame, the internal boundary, what it contains, what it hides,
and the audience's position relative to both. Explain the relation between the
nested frame and the complete field. A panel inside a large background matte should
not become a decorative card: state whether it magnifies evidence, interrupts
time, supplies memory, shows simultaneity, or stages a reaction.

Avoid adding windows, arches, borders, or letterbox bars because they look composed.
If removing the boundary changes no knowledge, feeling, route, or relationship,
remove it.
