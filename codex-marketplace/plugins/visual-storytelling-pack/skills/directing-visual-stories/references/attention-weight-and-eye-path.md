# Attention, visual weight, and eye path

Attention is authored by competing signals. Decide the intended read order before
adding polish: first, second, final, and optional discovery. The eye may roam, but
the composition should make the important relationship findable.

## Build a hierarchy

Weight can come from size, contrast, luminance, saturation, sharpness, isolation,
facial salience, recognisable silhouette, edge position, motion, repetition, or
directional convergence. Use a small number of strong differences rather than
making everything loud.

- **First read:** the subject or change the audience must not miss.
- **Second read:** the relationship, cause, reaction, or evidence that interprets it.
- **Final read:** a consequence, motif, route, or unanswered question that remains.
- **Discovery:** low-priority texture or optional information that rewards dwelling.

Leading vectors include gaze, gesture, body orientation, roads, architecture,
light, shadow, colour boundaries, text baselines, repeated forms, and motion paths.
Their endpoint matters: a line that leaves the field can create anticipation; one
that terminates on the subject can confirm the intended read. A vector that points
to an irrelevant edge is a leak.

## Contrast is relational

Reserve the strongest contrast for the story's active region. Contrast can separate
foreground from background, pair a subject with its consequence, or make a small
exception legible in a repeated field. Lower contrast can withhold, subordinate,
or let an environment breathe. Do not equate contrast with brightness alone;
texture, hue, scale, focus, motion, and silence also compete.

## Guard against noise

Detail density is a pacing tool. Dense areas invite search or establish complexity;
quiet areas provide rest, direction, or a place for a later reveal. Repetition
creates a baseline so that one exception reads as evidence. If every region has
unique detail, there is no hierarchy. If a focal point requires a label to be
found, strengthen the visual relation or revise the story beat.

## Attention route

Describe the path as a sequence of relations, not a rule: “gaze enters through the
bright doorway, follows the figure's sightline to the raised hand, then returns via
the red reflection to the missing person’s empty chair.” State what deliberately
interrupts that route and why. For a guided or exploratory field, identify at
least one alternate entry and the landmark that restores orientation.

## Check

Review a thumbnail, blurred reduction, or low-detail pass. Ask:

1. Is the first read the intended beat?
2. Does the second read explain rather than compete?
3. Can the eye leave and return through a designed vector?
4. Is any high-weight element accidental, decorative, or misleading?

If the answer fails, change hierarchy, placement, crop, or contrast—not merely the
amount of detail.
