# Spatial cinematography and environmental storytelling

Use this route when the space itself must orient, reveal history, or make the
audience infer what happened. Cinematography contributes controlled viewpoint,
depth, shot scale, focus, and movement. Games add agency: a navigable space must
work from many likely viewpoints and can change in response to action. The shared
principle is to make spatial evidence carry a beat.

## Compose a navigable read

Design a sequence of observation points rather than one ideal screenshot:

- **Arrival:** the audience understands where they are and what deserves notice.
- **Approach:** paths, framing, scale, occlusion, and distant landmarks create a
  reason to move.
- **Threshold:** crossing changes knowledge, mood, access, or social relation.
- **Dwell/interaction:** local evidence rewards attention without losing the larger
  landmark or route.
- **Return/departure:** changed state or a new view makes the journey meaningful.

Landmarks need relay hierarchy: silhouette, contrast, light, material, path shape,
terrain, motion, NPC gaze, and repeated motifs can agree without all becoming
arrows. A landmark that reads only from one camera angle is not enough for an
exploratory world. Leave recovery cues so the audience can reorient after search
or intentional disorientation.

Use compression and release as spatial pacing. Narrow passages, occlusion, low
ceilings, or clustered evidence compress attention; a vista, widening, height
change, or newly revealed landmark releases it. A vista is not just scenery: frame
the distant destination, establish its relation to the current route, and decide
whether the audience may approach it now or only anticipate it.

## Environmental evidence

Tell history through consequences that can be observed: wear, damage, repair,
occupation, abandoned tools, altered routes, traces, text, resource distribution,
and system response. Group evidence so the audience can infer a relationship or
prior event. A single prop may be more powerful than a pile of lore if it is placed
where the audience can connect it to action. Do not invent unsupported canon merely
to make a space feel full.

Deep staging can let a foreground action, middle-ground relationship, and
background consequence coexist. Focus or contrast can route the first read while
leaving other planes discoverable. Occlusion can delay evidence, but the audience
needs a fair path to understanding. Camera movement should reveal a relation,
follow a decision, or transfer attention—not merely tour assets.

## State and agency

The audience's action is part of the composition. Establish what the space affords,
then let a choice alter the field: a gate opens, a route becomes dangerous, a
market changes, a landmark lights, or a community occupies a repaired space. The
changed state should be spatially coherent, not a set of unrelated decoration
swaps. Repetition makes change legible: return to a familiar frame with one
meaningful difference and the audience supplies the history.

For web pages, scrolling and interaction can act as movement through a designed
space. A section threshold may disclose a new layer, alter light or density, or
reframe an earlier claim. The page remains authored when the route has landmarks,
orientation, and intentional release rather than a pile of sections.

### Route output

Record: audience agency and likely viewpoints; observation-point sequence; arrival,
threshold, dwell, return, and departure beats; landmark and relay cues; evidence
of prior action; state transitions; orientation/recovery plan; and what must remain
legible when the audience chooses a different route.

### Avoid

- designing a beautiful establishing view that fails during navigation;
- arrows, markers, or UI prompts doing all the spatial storytelling;
- prop clutter without causal, social, or historical evidence;
- invented backstory unsupported by the brief or project canon;
- resetting the world cosmetically after the audience has caused a change.
